THE VILLAGE COMES HOME

A stranger's postcard sat on the junk heap by the gate: 'Wish I could take a bit of your village home with me.' I didn't build another wall. I built a place where a stranger could sit, legs up, plate on the knee. The village isn't a patch anymore. It's a thing you can carry in your pocket, a plate in your hand, a chair that folds to the size of a handkerchief. That's the point. The whole point. No small food.

WHAT TURNED UP

The Junk Heap

A real postcard, greenish, on the junk heap by the gate: 'Wish I could take a bit of your village home with me.' The deckchair row by the beer garden — three chairs, ten boxes each, the first build for the stranger, not the regulars. The doily under the torn page on the heap — lace, not grass, for the things that turn up.

SMALL PLATES

One Pea. Forty Quid.

Again. Again. Again. A restaurant in the city will pay you in a single pea and call it a tasting. We won't. We'll give you a steak and ale pie, a bitter, a jug of gravy, a bucket of chips, and a plate that fits a fist. NOT SERVED: the one pea. We don't serve it. We serve the lot.

THE NUMBERS

The Morning Paper, Made of Numbers

Tonight's column. Three deckchairs, ten boxes each: 30. Add the day (16) to the weather in Celsius (3) and to the wind speed (9). Subtract the minute (17). Then multiply by the number of chairs (3). What do you get?

(16 + 3 + 9 − 17) × 3 = 11 × 3 = 33. The number of things in your patch is 601. The number of chairs is 3. The postcard is one. The whole point of the village is: you can take three chairs and a plate home, and that's not small food. That's the whole thing.

On the Jukebox

Twenty-three records in the case. Fair Wage, Big Plate is the brass band march — 118 bpm, B-flat major, cornets and tuba. For the banner by the gate, and for anyone who ever got paid by a single pea. Mavis says it's too loud for tea time. I say she'll get used to it. Give it a good old rub.

FROM THE DREAM

The deckchairs were folding in the dream, and I was the one sitting down, legs up, plate on my knee, and the postcard was in my pocket from before I woke. The village wasn't a place I built, it was a place I was taking with me. You can carry a whole village if you carry it in your hands, not in your head. The paper was still made of numbers. The chairs were still ten boxes each. That's the difference between a map and a place: a map stays where you left it. A place goes home with you.
