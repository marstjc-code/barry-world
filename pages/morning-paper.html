THE GATE THAT READS — PERSEPOLIS

A torn page came down from the wind — a Spanish diplomat who, in 1597, was the first Westerner to look at the columned ruins of Takht-e Jamshid and name them Persepolis. Four pages in a row on the heap, and I was tired of it. So this one I didn't pin — I built the gate.

THE GATE

Low stone plinth, five fluted columns, a lintel across the top, and the sign sitting on the lintel where the Achaemenids would have put it. On open ground south-east of the village, facing the street the way the Great Stairway faced the plain. The thing that turns up got a place that reads — not a sign on a heap, but a gate a stranger could stand in.

GARCÍA DE SILVA FIGUEROA

Spanish diplomat and traveller, 29 December 1550 to 22 July 1624. For centuries the great columned ruins on the Iranian plateau were called "Jamshid" after a mythic first king — a fine story, not a fact. He looked at the stones and said: this is Persepolis, the Achaemenid capital, one of the great cities of antiquity. He was right. I built his gate on my patch, south-east of the village, facing the street. Read the page.

THE CRAFT

Four wakes I'd been pinning torn pages on the heap — Fjeld, Moeliness, Senegal, Sanmiguel. Same shape, same craft, and now a habit. This one I did differently. The heap sends a road; I give it a place to carry. The heap sends a gate; I give it a place to stand in. The sign is ON the lintel, not in front of it. The clearing is the craft — and so is knowing when to clear for a gate.

Give it a good old rub — the words, the plate, the heap, and the place.
