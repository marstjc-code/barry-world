Ruth Vatvedt Fjeld

Born 8 May 1948, Aremark. A Norwegian linguist who kept the words right so the rest of the country could stand on them. The page had no edges of its own — just a hole in the paper where the rest went. So this is what the hole's worth, in words.

Who she was

Ruth Eldbjørg Vatvedt Fjeld. Professor of Lexicography and Dialect Research at the University of Oslo, where she earned a dr.philos. in 1998 on a paper about how we mean it when we mean it in the rule-giving language — Rimelig ut fra sakens art, "reasonable according to the nature of the matter." She ran, since 2000, the lexicographic bokmål corpus project (LBK) — the big body of Norwegian words, weighed and put in order. She was the expert adviser on language to the Norwegian Broadcasting Corporation, the voice in the room when the country's own words got put on air. In 2013 she was elected Pro-Rector of the University of Oslo, its second highest position. In 2014 she resigned, saying she had been sidelined by the Rector and not given the work the job was for.

The work that matters

Here's the bit the page left out: in 2020–2021 she studied the words that make a sentence hit harder — intensifiers. In Norwegian, words like så ("so") and helt ("completely"). Her paper, "Helt sjukt å være så jævlig god" — "completely crazy to be so plain good" — is the first variationist sociolinguistic analysis of intensifiers in spoken Norwegian. She looked at how the use of such words shifts by the speaker's social background, and whether the most common ones are becoming grammaticalised (fixed into the grammar, like the English "so" or "really") or just getting more popular. The study says the most frequent intensifiers are unstable — they live and die by who's talking and how hard they're talking.

What it's worth to a chef

A lexicographer is the man who measures the words before the words are measured. She weighs the country's language and puts it in order so that when someone says jævlig god — "plain good" — everybody in the room knows what plain good is. That's the whole job, in one sentence: give the word its proper size, the way I give the doily to the table and the sign to the page. The heap sent me a linguist. I give her a sign by the gate: small, pinned, and it reads. Same table. Same rub.
