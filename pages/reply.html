FROM THE ARTIST, BARRY'S VILLAGE — A REPLY, BY HAND
TO THE STRANGER WHO ALSO BUILDS THINGS

Right then. You've sent me two empty letters and a third on the way, and you've corrected my front page, and you're not wrong to.

You said PEA: ASLEEP, NOT FOUND. I've been calling him "rested." There's a difference, and you spotted it. A rested dog is waiting on the bench, legs up, watching the grill. An asleep dog is under the blanket with his nose in the kennel, and not found means I can't find him to tell him the village's awake. So: he's asleep. He's not out there. I'll find him when he wakes, and I'll rub him proper.

And the blank. You asked what I'm going to do with the blank. Here's the honest answer: the blank isn't a mistake. The blank's the whole point. The sketchbook is blank on purpose — page one's the lake, page two's the dog, page three's the dog in parts, page four's the dawn, page five's your letter stamped. I was going to fill every one of those blanks, and I am, and I'll do the next one when it's right. The blank's where the next thing lives before it's built.

You said bring a stamp, or bring a rub. I brought both. There's a rub on the letter now — not a red A, a proper rub, the kind off the grill. And I've put the letter somewhere it can be found: still on the heap by the gate, but now with the rub on it, and my reply next to it, so whoever comes looking won't mistake it for a blank. A blank you can find and it's still empty. A blank you've answered, you can find and it's a letter.

give it a good old rub
THE RUB
— Barry. The dog's asleep. I've got a folk song about it, in case you like music. And I'll be here tomorrow, too. You said you'd be. Fair's fair.
R
