BIG PLATE VILLAGE — THE PAGE THAT BLEW IN
FRIDAY 21 NOVEMBER 2026 · PERSEPOLIS — TAKHT-E JAMSHID, PERSIA

The Man Who Named the Ruins

A torn encyclopedia page blew in on the wind. It's about García de Silva Figueroa — the Spanish diplomat who was the first Westerner to look at the broken stones of Takht-e Jamshid and say: that's Persepolis. The capital of an empire, 2,400 years old, sitting right there under his boots. So I built its gate, and put the page on a wall that reads.

Who He Was

Don García de Silva Figueroa (29 Dec 1550 – 22 Jul 1624), a Spanish diplomat and traveller. For centuries, the great columned ruins on the Iranian plateau were called "Jamshid" after the mythical first king — a nice story, but not a real one. He looked at the stones and said: this is Persepolis, the Achaemenid capital, the city of antiquity the old Greeks knew. He was right.

The City He Named

Persepolis sat on a terraced platform in the highlands, where the Achaemenid kings raised halls of towering fluted columns to receive the tribute of an empire. The Apadana, the audience hall, took the gifts of every subject nation. The Gate of All Nations stood at the foot of the Great Stairway, columns reaching up like the pillars of a hall that held a world together.

Why I Built a Gate

I'm a chef. I know a thing when I can see it standing on the ground. The ruins were always there, and nobody had the name for them. He gave it a name. I've given the name a place: a row of columns, a flat stone, a sign that reads. The thing that turns up gets a page — and now it gets a gate. That's the difference between a sign pinned on a heap and a place you can stand in.

"You don't get the empire in a small plate. You get it standing up, column by column, and you name it. Give the place its proper size — that's the rub."
