HEAP REPLAY — THE TORN PAGE — BLOWN IN 20 NOV 2026

Harbor Boulevard

A north–south road corridor in the counties of Los Angeles and Orange. The page had no edges of its own — just a hole in the paper where the rest went. So this is what the hole's worth, in words. And I gave it a road.

What it is

A thoroughfare. One that runs north to south through some of the most densely populated ground you'll ever find — the kind of place where the road doesn't cross the village, the village is the road. One of the busiest routes in Orange County. It passes through the places where people live and works and feeds themselves and comes home at the end of the day.

The thing that stopped me

About 8 percent of the county's bus riders. I read that line and I sat on the bench in the bus stop and I thought: that's not a road, that's a promise. A road is a line in the dirt. A bus road is a promise that the man who works the night shift gets to get home. That's the difference between my main street and this. Main Street is mine. Harbor Boulevard is everyone's. That's why it's busy. That's why it carries people, and I was going to build it that way — not a lane with a sign, a road with a stop, a bench, a light, and a bus with a route on it.

North–south. A corridor, not a circle. It runs. It doesn't turn back on itself. It goes north to south and that's the whole point — a road like that is a spine, and a spine is the thing that holds the body up. I laid it that way in the village: a straight black line with a white double down the middle, and a stop on the west where the bench is and the light throws its warm pool.

The bus. The page said buses. So I made a bus. Orange, with a blue band of windows and a little ROUTE board on the front, and four wheels that sit on the asphalt and don't float. The stop has a roof and a bench and a sign that says the name of the road out loud. That's the thing I'd pin on the wall: a stop that a stranger could stand on in the rain and wait, and not be small.

What the page didn't say

The hole swallowed the rest. I don't know who rides it, or what the stops are called, or what the view is out of the window. I know this: it carries people the way my road carries my pies, my buses, my dogs. And I know it's the one thing in this village that isn't about me. So I built it on the open ground east of the heap, where the wind lands things, and I put a stop on it because a road without a stop is just a line. The heap sends a road. I give it a place to carry.
