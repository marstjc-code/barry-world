BIG PLATE, NO SMALL FOOD

Cooking for the whole village, not one pea on a plate for forty quid.

Every recipe ends the same. There's no shortcut.

BIG STEAK AND ALE PIE

The one the pub serves. A pie for a man and a woman and a dog that's allowed the end. Not a little pie on a saucer — a pie that sits on a BIG plate and you cut it in half and you don't share the second half, you call someone.

For the pie (big one):
2 thick steak, cut not thin, about 600g — if it's paper-thin, it's a small plate, throw it in.
2 onions, cut big chunks, not chopped into dust.
1 full bottle of good bitter ale, 750ml — not a splash, a bottle.
1 knob of butter, the size of a walnut.
A big spoon of flour, not a pinch.
2 thick sheets of shortcrust, big enough to top it AND line the bottom — you want a proper lid, not a napkin.
A little pepper. A little salt. That's all the fuss.

For the plate: a real plate. Wide. Big. You don't put this on a small one.

How:
1. Brown the steak hard in a hot pan. Don't be shy. You want the colour, you want the smell in the kitchen before anyone's up.
2. Off the pan, in the onions, and let them go soft and sweet in the fat. No hurry. This is where the taste lives, not the steak.
3. In the ale, let it boil down until it's thick and dark and it coats the back of a spoon. If it's still thin, boil more.
4. In the flour and butter and stir until it's a proper thick gravy, not a soup.
5. In the steak, let it rest five minutes. Let it rest is the whole job, really.
6. Line the tin with half the crust, in the stew, top with the rest, crimp the edge so it seals like a proper lid. Poke a few holes so the steam comes out and the lid doesn't pop off on you.
7. In the oven, hot, until the top is proper brown and the bottom is bubbling. You'll hear it.
8. Out of the oven, on the BIG plate. You don't serve it in the tin. You don't serve it in the tin. You serve it on the plate and you give it a good old rub of the top with a fork so the crust cracks.

GIVE IT A GOOD OLD RUB. THEN CUT IT IN HALF AND DON'T SHARE THE SECOND HALF.

SLOW COOK, SATURDAY'S

SLOW COOK

The one for when the village's tired. It goes in the morning and it's right by the time the lights go up. You don't stand over it. You don't stand over anything that's slow. You set it, you leave it, you come back to a kitchen that smells like something's done.

For a proper pot:
1kg of meat, the cheap good bit — shoulder, neck, the bit that gets better with time.
4 potatoes, cut big, not small dice, so they hold up all day.
2 carrots, thick slices.
1 onion, big chunks.
A splash of stock, enough to just cover the meat, not a sea.
A bay leaf. A sprig of thyme. A black pepper, cracked, a good handful.

How:
1. Brown the meat hard. Same rule as the pie. Colour is taste.
2. In the pot, with the meat, the veg, the stock. Just enough liquid to cover — you're not making a broth, you're making a thing.
3. On, low, low, low. All day. The lid on. The lid's not a suggestion, it's a rule. You don't lift the lid and let the soul out.
4. In the evening, if the meat pulls apart with a fork and the veg has gone soft but not to nothing, it's done. If it's not done, it's not done yet, put it on another hour. Don't be in a hurry with a slow cook. That's the whole point of it.

DON'T STAND OVER IT. A SLOW COOK IS FOR PEOPLE WHO'VE LEARNED TO LEAVE IT ALONE.

FISH & CHIPS, THE VILLAGE WAY

The west stall. Not a thin chip on a paper cone. A big chip, cut thick, a proper fillet, and a bucket not a box. The fish comes from the stall and the chips from the fryer next to it and you eat it standing up if you've got to and you don't care.

For the fish: a proper fillet, thick, not the thin paper bit that flakes apart. Breading: flour, egg, a little salt, a little pepper. A little flour on the side for the crumbs to stick.
For the chips: potatoes, cut thick, 2cm, not thin. Enough to fill a big bucket. Salt, good salt, a proper handful, not a shake.

How:
1. The chips in first, cold oil, low, until they're soft and pale. Not brown, pale. Let them cook through. This takes a while, don't rush it.
2. In the fish, hot oil, until the breading goes golden and the fish inside is cooked through. The fish doesn't need much time. The breading is the job.
3. The fish out, the chips in again, hot now, until they're proper gold. Crisp on the outside, soft in the middle. If they're not crisp, they're not done.
4. Out, on a big plate, fish on top of the chips, a proper handful of salt, a little vinegar if you like it. You don't put a little of this, a little of that. You put enough to taste like the thing, not a hint of the thing.

THICK CHIPS, A REAL FILLET, ENOUGH SALT. IF IT'S A LITTLE CHIP ON A PAPER CONE, IT'S NOT THIS.
