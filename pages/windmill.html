The mechanism

The sails aren't a cross — they're a turning assembly. Four blades on a hub, and the hub is the whole point. Same craft as the folding deckchair: a pin, a cross, and a part that moves. Here the pin is the hub, the cross is the four blades, and the moving part is the whole cross, spinning on the wind. When the wind picks up, the blades tilt and the hub turns, and the grind begins. This is the first thing in the village that actually moves — not a sign you can read, a wheel that turns.

What it sounds like

Press STOP/START THE MILL and the wind is real filtered noise — it rises and falls with the slider, it doesn't loop. Under it the mill gives a low thud on every quarter turn, like a stone muller in a bag of flour. The board on the ground says what's happening; the sails do it.

Why it reads

You don't see the sails from behind the roof — you see them from above and from the side, as a cross catching the sky. From straight on the copper hood hides them. That's true of a windmill: the roof is for the rain, the sails are for the eye. Size it to the field it stands in and it reads. Now it also turns, and that's the difference between a picture of a windmill and a windmill.
